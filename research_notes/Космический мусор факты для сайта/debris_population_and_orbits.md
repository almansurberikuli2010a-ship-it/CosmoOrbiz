## 1. How many objects are tracked and catalogued today, and how many are active satellites vs debris?

### Takeaway
ESA counts about 47,260 objects tracked and catalogued as of 23 October 2026, and about 15,900 of the satellites are still working. So only about one tracked object in three is a working satellite. NASA's public US catalogue is smaller: 34,054 objects as of 4 August 2026. That total is 19,154 spacecraft (active and dead) plus 14,900 rocket bodies and other debris. The difference comes from catalogue scope, not from a contradiction. The tracked population has grown fast: about 35,000 in mid-2024, about 40,000 in April 2025, and about 47,260 in October 2026.

### Cited Findings
**ESA "Space debris by the numbers" ("Information last updated on 23 October 2026")** — [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/Space_debris_by_the_numbers); [data embed](https://sdup.esoc.esa.int/discosweb/statistics/embed/bythenumbers)
- Rocket launches since the start of the space age in 1957: "About 7370 (excluding failures)".
- Satellites these launches placed into Earth orbit: "About 28270".
  - Still in space: "About 19440".
  - Still functioning: "About 15900".
- Space objects "regularly tracked by Space Surveillance Networks and maintained in their catalogue": "About 47260".
- Break-ups, explosions, collisions or anomalous events resulting in fragmentation: "More than 660".

**ESA Space Environment Report 2026 (PDF Issue 10.1, 8 Sept 2026; status "as of end of 2025")** — [ESA PDF](https://www.sdo.esoc.esa.int/environment_report/Space_Environment_Report_I10R1_20260908.pdf)
- Table 3.1, "Number of objects orbiting Earth", gives a total of 44,964 catalogued and asserted objects at end of 2025. By type:
  - 16,946 payloads (PL, active and inactive)
  - 2,080 rocket bodies (RB)
  - 7,498 rocket fragmentation debris (RF)
  - 5,975 payload fragmentation debris (PF)
  - 11,087 unidentified (UI)
  - 393 payload mission-related objects (PM)
  - 772 rocket mission-related objects (RM)
  - 144 payload debris (PD)
  - 69 rocket debris (RD)
- Executive summary: "Ever since the start of the space age there has been more space debris in orbit than operational satellites."
- 2025 activity: "1200 intact objects re-entering in 2025" (p. 9). Over the last two decades, "9.8 non-deliberate fragmentations continue to occur in the space environment every year" (p. 5).
  - 2024 saw "a substantial increase of over 3000 catalogued fragments mostly attributed to propulsion-related breakups" (p. 5).
  - Table 3.7 lists 3 fragmentation events in 2025.

**ESA Space Environment Report 2026 web page (14/09/2026)** — [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/ESA_Space_Environment_Report_2026)

- "Even if no new satellites were launched, the debris population would continue to increase."

- "Intact satellites or rocket bodies are now re-entering the Earth atmosphere on average more than three times a day."

**ESA Space Environment Report 2024 web page (19/07/2024; describes 2023)** — [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/ESA_Space_Environment_Report_2024)

**NASA ODPO FAQ** — [NASA](https://orbitaldebris.jsc.nasa.gov/faq/)
- "As of August 2026, the U.S. Space Surveillance Network had cataloged more than 34,000 objects in Earth orbit, including active and defunct spacecraft, spent rocket bodies, fragmentation debris, and other cataloged debris."
- Also (undated on page): "Approximately 50,000 large, trackable objects orbit Earth, along with an estimated 100 million pieces of millimeter-sized orbital debris."
- The SSN "routinely tracks larger orbital debris, generally objects greater than 10 centimeters" (undated).

### Inferences
- Active vs not active, using ESA's 23 Oct 2026 numbers: 15,900 functioning ÷ 47,260 tracked ≈ 34%. So about 66% of tracked objects (≈31,360) are not working satellites: dead satellites, rocket bodies, fragments and mission-related objects.
  - ESA's figures also imply about 3,540 dead satellites still in orbit (19,440 − 15,900).
- Earlier ESA ratios: about 26% active in the 2024 report (9,100 of 35,000) and about 27.5% in the 2025 report (11,000 of 40,000). The active share is rising because of mega-constellations, but debris still dominates.
- In NASA's box score, spacecraft (active and defunct) are 56% of catalogued objects (19,154 of 34,054). Rocket bodies and debris are 44%. This box score does not separate active from dead spacecraft.
- Why ESA (~47,260) and NASA (~34,054) differ: ESA counts objects "regularly tracked by Space Surveillance Networks and maintained in their catalogue". The ESA report merges USSTRATCOM, Vimpel/KIAM and RAE data plus asserted objects. NASA's box score counts only objects officially entered in the public U.S. Satellite Catalog. NASA's own FAQ separately says "approximately 50,000 large, trackable objects". The website should name the source when quoting either number.
- Suggested website wording (derived):
  - "About 47,000 human-made objects are tracked in Earth orbit (ESA, Oct 2026). Only about 16,000 of them are working satellites. The rest are space junk."
  - "Since 1957 there have been more than 660 explosions, break-ups and collisions in orbit (ESA, Oct 2026)."

### Gaps
- The ESA 2026 web page does not restate the catalogue size. The "~47,260" figure comes from the continuously updated "by the numbers" page (23 Oct 2026), not from the report itself. The report's own end-2025 table total is 44,964.
- ESA's 2024 web page does not state its data cut-off explicitly. It describes 2023 activity, so treat its numbers as "2023/2024".
- The NASA box score does not split active from defunct spacecraft. No NASA primary figure for "active satellites" was found.

## 2. Statistical (MASTER) estimates of objects by size, and their date

### Takeaway
ESA's MASTER-8 model (reference population 1 February 2026) estimates:
- 68,450 objects larger than 10 cm, including about 11,300 active payloads
- 1.5 million debris objects from 1 to 10 cm
- 230 million debris objects from 1 mm to 1 cm

The same figures appear in both the "by the numbers" page and the 2026 report.

### Cited Findings
- ESA "Space debris by the numbers" (updated 23 Oct 2026): "Not all objects are tracked and catalogued. The number of objects estimated based on statistical models to be in orbit (MASTER-8, reference population 02/2026)". The three figures are:
  - "68450 space objects greater than 10 cm (including approx. 11300 active payloads)"
  - "1.5 million space debris objects from greater than 1 cm to 10 cm"
  - "230 million space debris objects from greater than 1 mm to 1 cm"

  — [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/Space_debris_by_the_numbers); [data embed](https://sdup.esoc.esa.int/discosweb/statistics/embed/bythenumbers)
- ESA Space Environment Report 2026, Section 2.4, p. 31: "at the most recent reference epoch 1st February 2026, the estimated number of space objects in orbit" is:
  - "68,450 space objects greater than 10 cm (including approximately 11,300 active payloads)"
  - "1.5 million space debris objects from 1 cm to 10 cm"
  - "230 million space debris objects from 1 mm to 1 cm"

  — [ESA PDF](https://www.sdo.esoc.esa.int/environment_report/Space_Environment_Report_I10R1_20260908.pdf)
- The previous MASTER reference populations named in the same report are 1 August 2024 and 1 November 2016 (p. 32). Since the 2024 population, MASTER also publishes a "non-manoeuvrable" population that excludes active, manoeuvrable spacecraft (p. 32). — [ESA PDF](https://www.sdo.esoc.esa.int/environment_report/Space_Environment_Report_I10R1_20260908.pdf)
- Older ESA headline values:
  - 2025 report page (01/04/2025): objects larger than 1 cm "estimated to be over 1.2 million, with over 50.000 objects of those larger than 10 cm" — [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/ESA_Space_Environment_Report_2025)
  - 2024 report page (19/07/2024): objects larger than 1 cm "is over one million" — [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/ESA_Space_Environment_Report_2024)
  - ESA, 04/04/2017: "around 750 000 objects larger than 1 cm" — [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/European_conference_on_space_debris_risks_and_mitigation)
- NASA's different, model-based figure: "an estimated 100 million pieces of millimeter-sized orbital debris" (NASA ODPO FAQ, undated). This does not match ESA's 230 million for 1 mm–1 cm. The two agencies use different models (NASA ORDEM vs ESA MASTER) and different size wording. — [NASA](https://orbitaldebris.jsc.nasa.gov/faq/)

### Inferences
- Of the ~68,450 objects larger than 10 cm, about 57,150 are not active payloads (68,450 − 11,300). That is roughly five pieces of large junk for every working satellite in this size class (Feb 2026 epoch).
- 47,260 tracked (Oct 2026) ÷ 68,450 modelled above 10 cm (Feb 2026) ≈ 69%. This suggests roughly a third of the >10 cm population is still not in catalogues. This is a rough comparison only: the dates differ, and catalogues also contain some objects smaller than 10 cm.
- Each step down in size multiplies the count about 20–150 times: ~68 thousand above 10 cm, 1.5 million at 1–10 cm, 230 million at 1 mm–1 cm. This is a good "iceberg" visual for the website.
- Suggested wording: "Scientists estimate there are about 1.5 million pieces of debris between 1 and 10 cm and about 230 million between 1 mm and 1 cm. Most are too small to track (ESA MASTER-8 model, February 2026)."

### Gaps
- The ESA 2025 page does not say which MASTER epoch its "over 1.2 million / over 50,000" figures come from. Presumably it is the 1 Aug 2024 population (**UNCERTAIN**).
- Earlier snapshots of the "by the numbers" page (2024–2025) were not retrieved, so no verified year-by-year series of MASTER numbers is given here.

## 3. Total mass of human-made objects in Earth orbit

### Takeaway
There are more than 17,400 tonnes of human-made objects in Earth orbit (ESA, updated 23 October 2026). ESA's end-of-2025 report tables give 15,883 t, but only for objects of known mass. More than half of that mass is in LEO (8,750 t) and about 2,850 t is in GEO.

### Cited Findings
- "Total mass of all space objects in Earth orbit: More than 17400 tonnes" (ESA "by the numbers", updated 23 Oct 2026). — [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/Space_debris_by_the_numbers); [data embed](https://sdup.esoc.esa.int/discosweb/statistics/embed/bythenumbers)
- ESA Space Environment Report 2026, Table 3.3, "Mass in tonnes orbiting Earth", status end of 2025 — [ESA PDF](https://www.sdo.esoc.esa.int/environment_report/Space_Environment_Report_I10R1_20260908.pdf)
  - Total: 15,883.0 t, of which payloads 11,898.5 t and rocket bodies 3,838.0 t.
  - By orbit: LEO 8,749.9 t; GEO 2,846.8 t; extended GEO (EGO) 1,462.3 t.
  - The table notes: "Objects of unknown mass do not contribute to the figures presented." The report explains that debris and unidentified objects "have no mass nor area indicated" (Section 1.2). That is why this total is lower than the 17,400 t headline.
- NASA ODQN Vol. 30 Issue 3 includes a chart titled "Monthly Mass of All Cataloged Objects in Earth Orbit by Object Type as of 24 August 2026". The y-axis runs to 18,000 metric tons, but the text gives no single numeric total. — [NASA PDF](https://orbitaldebris.jsc.nasa.gov/quarterly-news/pdfs/ODQNv30i3.pdf)

### Inferences
- Using ESA's tables, about 55% of the known orbiting mass is in LEO (8,749.9 of 15,883.0 t) and about 18% in GEO (2,846.8 t).
- Suggested wording: "Humans have left more than 17,400 tonnes of hardware in Earth orbit (ESA, October 2026)."

### Gaps
- NASA's August 2026 mass total is available only as a chart, with no exact number. No NASA numeric mass figure was found.

## 4. Collision speeds in LEO and the damage/energy of a 1 cm fragment

### Takeaway
NASA says LEO debris travels at 7–8 km/s. Collisions happen at an average relative speed of about 10 km/s and can reach about 15 km/s, "more than 10 times the speed of a bullet". ESA says a 1 cm fragment at about 10 km/s releases about as much energy as a small car crashing at 40 km/h (2021). ESA has also compared it to an exploding hand grenade (2017). Our own kinetic-energy check supports the car comparison.

### Cited Findings
- NASA ODPO FAQ (Q8, undated):
  - "In low Earth orbit, below 1,243 miles (2,000 kilometers), orbital debris travels at speeds of about 15,659 to 17,896 mph (7 to 8 kilometers per second)."
  - "Orbital debris strikes other space objects at an average relative speed of approximately … (10 kilometers per second) and can reach relative speeds of about … (15 kilometers per second, more than 10 times the speed of a bullet."
  - **Warning:** the live page prints "6.2 mph" and "9.3 mph". These are unit typos for miles **per second** (10 km/s ≈ 6.2 mi/s). Do not copy the mph values.

  — [NASA](https://orbitaldebris.jsc.nasa.gov/faq/)
- ESA image "The impact of space debris", page dated 24/03/2021: "Debris objects travel at about 10 km/s, meaning a collision with just 1cm fragment can create the same amount of energy as a small car crashing at 40 km/h!" — [ESA](https://www.esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Images/2021/03/The_impact_of_space_debris)
- ESA press page, 04/04/2017: "At average speeds of 40 000 km/h, impacts on space hardware would deliver roughly the energy equivalent to the explosion of a hand grenade" (said of objects larger than 1 cm). — [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/European_conference_on_space_debris_risks_and_mitigation)
- ESA 2024 report page (19/07/2024): objects "larger than 1 cm in size – large enough to be capable of causing catastrophic damage". — [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/ESA_Space_Environment_Report_2024)
- NASA ODPO FAQ (Q10, undated):
  - ISS shielding lets critical components "normally withstand impacts from debris as large as 1 centimeter in diameter".
  - "Debris between 1 and 10 centimeters in diameter poses a slight risk of striking a critical station component."
  - Q15: millimeter-sized debris "represents the highest mission-ending penetration risk" to most LEO robotic missions.

  — [NASA](https://orbitaldebris.jsc.nasa.gov/faq/)
- NASA ODPO FAQ (Q12, undated) on how long debris stays up:
  - Below 600 km: "normally falls back to Earth within several years".
  - At 800 km: "orbital decay often takes centuries".
  - Above 1,000 km: "normally continues orbiting Earth for 1,000 years or more".

  — [NASA](https://orbitaldebris.jsc.nasa.gov/faq/)
- An ESA-hosted conference paper (JAXA authors, 9th European Conference on Space Debris, SDC9) states "10–15 km/s of LEO" as the typical LEO relative velocity range, compared with "less than 1–2 km/s" in GEO. — [Harada, Kawamoto, Hanada, SDC9 paper 118](https://conference.sdo.esoc.esa.int/proceedings/sdc9/paper/118/SDC9-paper118.pdf)

### Inferences
These are our own physics calculations.
- Head-on speed: two objects in circular orbits at 550 km each move at about 7.59 km/s (v = √(GM/r)), so a head-on hit is about 15 km/s. Crossing at 90° gives √2 × 7.59 ≈ 10.7 km/s. This matches NASA's "~10 km/s average, up to ~15 km/s".
- 1 cm fragment: an aluminium sphere 1 cm across (density 2.70 g/cm³) weighs about 1.41 g.
  - At 10 km/s its kinetic energy is about 71 kJ; at 15 km/s about 159 kJ.
  - A 1,000 kg car at 40 km/h has about 62 kJ. So ESA's "small car at 40 km/h" comparison is physically consistent.
- 10 cm fragment: an aluminium sphere 10 cm across (≈1.41 kg) at 10 km/s carries about 71 MJ, about the energy of 17 kg of TNT (using 4.184 MJ/kg). This shows why ESA and NASA treat 10 cm objects as able to destroy a satellite.
- Suggested wording:
  - "In low Earth orbit, debris hits at about 10 km/s on average, and up to 15 km/s head-on. That is more than 10 times faster than a bullet (NASA)."
  - "A 1 cm fragment at 10 km/s hits with as much energy as a small car crashing at 40 km/h (ESA)."

### Gaps
- **UNCERTAIN:** ESA gives no calculation behind the "hand grenade" comparison. By our estimate, 71–87 kJ for 1.4 g at 10–11 km/s is probably below the chemical energy of a typical hand grenade, but no grenade figure was sourced here. Use the car comparison, or present the grenade as ESA's rough analogy.
- **UNCERTAIN:** a 2015 ESA Clean Space item reportedly says "even a 1 cm nut could hit a satellite with the force of a hand grenade" ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Clean_Space/ESA_experts_assess_risk_from_exploded_satellite)). This was seen in a search snippet only and not opened.
- **UNCERTAIN:** the 2009 Iridium-33/Cosmos-2251 relative velocity (~11.6–11.7 km/s) appeared only in a search snippet. It was not verified against a NASA ODQN primary.

## 5. Orbital physics: GEO vs LEO altitudes and speeds, why GEO relative velocities are low, and the graveyard orbit

### Takeaway
| | GEO | LEO |
|---|---|---|
| Altitude | 35,786 km above the equator | below 2,000 km |
| Speed | about 3 km/s (3.07 km/s calculated) | about 7.8 km/s (ESA) |
| Period | one sidereal day (23 h 56 min 4 s) | about 90 minutes |

GEO satellites move in nearly the same plane and the same direction at the same speed, so their relative velocities are small: "less than 1–2 km/s", versus 10–15 km/s in LEO. At end of life, GEO satellites should be boosted into a "graveyard" orbit. The IADC formula requires at least 235 km + 1000·C_R·A/m above GEO. In practice this is about 250–300 km, which is the source of the popular "~300 km" figure.

### Cited Findings
- ESA "Types of orbits" (page dated 30/03/2020) — [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Space_Transportation/Types_of_orbits)
  - GEO satellites "fly above Earth's equator, moving from west to east, exactly matching Earth's rotation: taking 23 hours 56 minutes and 4 seconds to complete one full orbit, i.e. the duration of a sidereal day."
  - "they travel at about 3 km per second at an altitude of 35 786 km".
  - LEO: "LEO is considered to be under altitudes of 2000 km", and "Generally, satellites do not fly below 180 km".
  - "Satellites in low-Earth orbit travel at approximately 7.8 km per second with respect to Earth, completing an orbit in about 90 minutes. This means the ISS circles Earth roughly 16 times a day."
- Protected regions, ESA Space Environment Report 2026, Table 1.3 (8 Sept 2026):
  - "IADC LEO Protected Region h ∈ [0, 2000]" km.
  - "IADC GEO Protected Region h ∈ [35586, 35986]" km, declination "δ ∈ [−15, 15]" degrees, i.e. GEO altitude ±200 km and ±15° latitude.

  — [ESA PDF](https://www.sdo.esoc.esa.int/environment_report/Space_Environment_Report_I10R1_20260908.pdf)
- IADC Space Debris Mitigation Guidelines, IADC-02-01 Rev. 4 (January 2025; revision dated 2025-01-16), §5.3.1 Geosynchronous Region — [UNOOSA PDF](https://www.unoosa.org/res/oosadoc/data/documents/2025/aac_105c_12025crp/aac_105c_12025crp_9_0_html/AC105_C1_2025_CRP09E.pdf)
  - Spacecraft and stages ending their mission in GEO "should manoeuvre immediately away from GEO". They should go into "an orbit that remains outside the GEO Protected Region for at least 100 years".
  - Condition 1: "A minimum increase in perigee altitude of: 235 km + (1000 × C_R × A/m)". C_R is "the solar radiation pressure coefficient, typically in the range of about 1.2 to 1.5" and A/m is "the aspect area to dry mass ratio (m² kg⁻¹)".
  - "235 km is the sum of the upper altitude of the GEO Protected Region (200 km) and the maximum descent of a re-orbited spacecraft due to luni-solar & geopotential perturbations (35 km)".
  - Condition 2: "An eccentricity less than or equal to 0.003."
  - LEO rule in the same document: residual orbital lifetime "no more than a maximum of 25 years". Post-mission disposal success "should be at least 90% with a goal of 99% or better".
- Recommendation ITU-R S.1003-2 (12/2010), "Environmental protection of the geostationary-satellite orbit" — [UNOOSA copy](https://www.unoosa.org/documents/pdf/spacelaw/sd/R-REC-S1003-2-201012-IPDF-E.pdf)
  - A GEO satellite at end of life "should be removed from the GSO region such that … it would subsequently remain in an orbit with a perigee no less than 200 km above the geostationary altitude".
  - Its Annex gives "ΔH > 235 + 1 000 Cr A/M … for eccentricities < 0.003".
  - Context (2010): "approximately 1 100 known spacecraft and rocket bodies near the GSO, of which approximately one-third are currently operational".
- GEO disposal compliance, ESA Space Environment Report 2026 (8 Sept 2026): "Between 85% and 100% of all payloads reaching end-of-life during the last decade in the GEO protected region attempt to comply with space debris mitigation measures. Between 70% and 90% do so successfully". The same report quotes the IADC formula ΔH = 235 + (1000·Cr·A/m) as the "graveyard orbit" criterion (p. 135). — [ESA PDF](https://www.sdo.esoc.esa.int/environment_report/Space_Environment_Report_I10R1_20260908.pdf)
- NASA ODPO FAQ (Q14, undated) — [NASA](https://orbitaldebris.jsc.nasa.gov/faq/)
  - "The amount of orbital debris in geosynchronous orbit is generally less severe than in low Earth orbit."
  - "spacecraft operators typically move spacecraft nearing the end of their missions into disposal graveyard orbits above the geosynchronous region."
- Why GEO relative speeds are low: "Since most GEO objects have an orbital inclination close to 0 degrees, the relative velocity of each object is less than 1–2 km/s, which is smaller than 10–15 km/s of LEO". The same paper notes meteoroids dominate GEO impacts for particles smaller than 1 cm. — [Harada, Kawamoto, Hanada (JAXA/Kyushu Univ.), SDC9 paper 118](https://conference.sdo.esoc.esa.int/proceedings/sdc9/paper/118/SDC9-paper118.pdf)
- NASA tracks GEO debris below the SSN tracking threshold with ground-based optical telescopes, for example ES-MCAT (ODQN Vol. 30 Issue 3, Sept 2026). — [NASA PDF](https://orbitaldebris.jsc.nasa.gov/quarterly-news/pdfs/ODQNv30i3.pdf)

### Inferences
These are our own calculations, using GM = 398,600.4 km³/s² and Earth radius 6,378.1 km.
- **GEO speed:** r = 42,164 km, so v = √(GM/r) = 3.075 km/s. The same follows from 2πr divided by a sidereal day of 86,164 s, which gives 3.07 km/s. So "3.07 km/s" is correct; ESA rounds it to "about 3 km/s".
- **LEO speeds and periods:**

  | Altitude | Speed | Period |
  |---|---|---|
  | 200 km | 7.78 km/s | 88.5 min |
  | 400 km (ISS) | 7.67 km/s | 92.6 min |
  | 550 km (Starlink shells) | 7.59 km/s | 95.6 min |
  | 800 km | 7.45 km/s | 100.9 min |

  So "7.5–7.8 km/s" is correct across LEO. ESA's "7.8 km/s" applies to the lowest orbits. LEO satellites move about 2.5 times faster than GEO satellites.
- **Why GEO is gentler:** the relative speed of two satellites on circular orbits of the same radius is v_rel = 2·v·sin(θ/2), where θ is the angle between their orbital planes.
  - Station-kept GEO satellites share the equatorial plane, direction and speed, so θ ≈ 0 and they barely move relative to one another.
  - An object inclined 15° (the latitude edge of the GEO protected region) crossing an equatorial satellite gives 2 × 3.075 × sin 7.5° ≈ 0.8 km/s. That is consistent with the "less than 1–2 km/s" statement.
  - In LEO, orbits cross at large angles in all directions and the speed is ~2.5 times higher. That gives ~10 km/s typical and ~15 km/s head-on.
- **Graveyard altitude:** with C_R = 1.2–1.5 (IADC) and an assumed A/m of 0.01–0.04 m²/kg, the formula gives ΔH ≈ 247–295 km. The A/m range is our assumption for typical GEO comsats (**UNCERTAIN**).
  - Minimum graveyard perigee is therefore about 35,786 + 235 ≈ 36,021 km, and typically about 36,040–36,080 km.
  - "About 300 km above GEO" is a fair rounded website figure, as long as it is attributed to the IADC formula rather than stated as a fixed IADC number.
- Suggested wording:
  - "Geostationary satellites orbit 35,786 km above the equator at about 3 km/s, taking exactly one (sidereal) day per orbit."
  - "Low-orbit satellites race around at 7.5–7.8 km/s, once every ~90 minutes."
  - "Retired GEO satellites are pushed roughly 300 km higher into a 'graveyard orbit' (IADC guideline: at least 235 km plus a margin for solar pressure)."

### Gaps
- **UNCERTAIN:** a widely cited "300 km" re-orbit figure is said to come from the original 1993 ITU-R S.1003-0 ("300 km or more"). This came only from a search summary of the 1993 text ([ITU](https://itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/s/R-REC-S.1003-0-199304-S!!PDF-F.pdf)), which was not opened. The current ITU text (2010) says a perigee of at least 200 km plus the 235 km formula.
- **UNCERTAIN:** a 2017–18 study by Oltrogge et al. reportedly found GEO collision likelihood and relative velocities higher than earlier estimates. This was seen only via a Physics World snippet ([Physics World](https://physicsworld.com/a/space-debris-threat-to-geosynchronous-satellites-has-been-drastically-underestimated/)), not the paper. Avoid saying GEO collisions are "harmless".
- No authoritative primary gives the classic "~0–15° inclination drift cycle of dead GEO satellites". The 15° used above is the protected-region latitude limit, not a sourced drift amplitude.

## 6. How many satellites are in LEO vs GEO, and Starlink's share of active satellites

### Takeaway
LEO holds the vast majority of satellites. In ESA's end-2025 tables, 14,906 of 16,946 payloads (88%) are in LEO and 799 (4.7%) are in the GEO band. Both counts include dead payloads. Starlink alone had 11,151 working satellites on 26 October 2026 (McDowell). That is about 70% of all functioning satellites (ESA: ~15,900 on 23 Oct 2026).

### Cited Findings
- ESA Space Environment Report 2026, Table 3.1 (status end of 2025) — [ESA PDF](https://www.sdo.esoc.esa.int/environment_report/Space_Environment_Report_I10R1_20260908.pdf)
  - LEO: 25,060 objects in total, of which 14,906 payloads.
  - GEO (i ≤ 25°, perigee and apogee 35,586–35,986 km): 961 objects, of which 799 payloads.
  - EGO (extended geostationary orbit, which includes disposal/graveyard regions): 5,358 objects, of which 571 payloads.
  - All orbits: 44,964 objects, of which 16,946 payloads.
  - Table 3.2: 29,175 objects intersect the LEO protected region and 9,667 intersect the GEO protected region (absolute counts).
  - The report's payload class includes active and inactive payloads.
  - 2025 additions (Table 3.8): 4,353 new payloads in LEO vs 24 in GEO (4,409 payloads in total).
- Jonathan McDowell, Starlink statistics ("Data last updated: 2026 Oct 26 0051:30") — [planet4589.org](https://planet4589.org/space/con/star/stats.html)
  - Total launched: 13,042.
  - Total in orbit: 11,166. Total working: 11,151. In operational orbit: 10,022.
  - Total down: 1,876, which includes 1,523 "Disposal Complete", 152 "Reentry after Fail", 181 "Early Deorbit" and 20 "Failed to orbit".
- Jonathan McDowell, "Active Sats versus Orbit Type" ("Data as of 2026 Oct 12"): "Number of small (<100 kg) sats: 1600.6" and "Number of big (>100 kg) sats: 14248.2", about 15,850 active in total. — [planet4589.org](https://planet4589.org/space/stats/oactive.html)
- ESA "by the numbers" (23 Oct 2026): satellites "still functioning: About 15900". — [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/Space_debris_by_the_numbers)
- ESA 2024 report page (19/07/2024): "Two-thirds of all active satellites, over 6 000, are currently located between altitudes of 500 and 600 km." — [ESA](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/ESA_Space_Environment_Report_2024)
- Older operational-satellite split (UCS Satellite Database, "Data current through: 5/1/23") — [UCS](https://www.ucs.org/resources/satellite-database)
  - "Total number of operating satellites: 7,560": LEO 6,768; MEO 143; Elliptical 59; GEO 590.
  - UCS states its database "has paused updates", so these are 2023 figures.
- ITU-R S.1003-2 (2010): about 1,100 spacecraft and rocket bodies near GSO, about one-third operational. — [UNOOSA copy](https://www.unoosa.org/documents/pdf/spacelaw/sd/R-REC-S1003-2-201012-IPDF-E.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Starlink share of working satellites:** 11,151 ÷ 15,900 ≈ 70%, using McDowell (26 Oct 2026) and ESA (23 Oct 2026). With McDowell's own active total (~15,850, 12 Oct 2026) it is also ≈ 70%.
- **Starlink share of all satellites in orbit (including dead ones):** 11,166 ÷ 19,440 ≈ 57%, using McDowell's count and ESA's "still in space" figure.
- **LEO vs GEO:** by ESA's end-2025 payload count (active + inactive), LEO holds about 88% of payloads and the GEO band about 5%. The 2023 UCS split of operating satellites was LEO 89.5%, GEO 7.8%.
  - GEO's operating fleet (~590 in 2023) is small and grows slowly: only 24 new GEO payloads in 2025 vs 4,353 in LEO.
  - LEO numbers are exploding because of constellations.
- McDowell's active total (~15,850, 12 Oct 2026) and ESA's functioning count (~15,900, 23 Oct 2026) agree within about 0.3%. This is a useful cross-check for the website's "about 16,000 working satellites".
- Suggested wording:
  - "About 7 out of every 10 working satellites today are SpaceX Starlink satellites: 11,151 of about 15,900 (Oct 2026)."
  - "Almost 9 in 10 satellites fly in low Earth orbit. Only about 5% sit in the geostationary belt."

### Gaps
- **No authoritative 2026 count of *active* GEO satellites was found.**
  - UCS (590, May 2023) is the latest primary-quality figure, but it is three years old.
  - ESA's 799 GEO payloads (end-2025) include dead ones.
  - Commercial trackers seen only in search snippets (Orbital Radar: "1,246 active" in GEO, undated; KeepTrack: 1,421 in GEO, Oct 2026) use unclear definitions and were not verified. **UNCERTAIN, not recommended.** — [Orbital Radar GEO directory](https://orbitalradar.com/satellite-directory/orbit/geo); [KeepTrack live count](https://keeptrack.space/live-count/how-many-satellites-are-in-space)
- McDowell's "Active Sats versus Orbit Type" per-orbit columns look inconsistent for GEO. They list only ~40 active "GEO" satellites as of 12 Oct 2026, and the HTML headers have 8 columns while the data file has 10. They were therefore **not used** for orbit splits; only his totals were used.
- ESA's ~15,900 "still functioning" (Oct 2026) and the MASTER-8 "approx. 11300 active payloads" (Feb 2026 epoch) differ because of date and definition. Do not mix them in one sentence.
