# Active Debris Removal (ADR) and On-Orbit Servicing: What Has Actually Been Achieved (focus: Astroscale), status as of 26 October 2026

Conventions used in these notes:
- Dates are UTC unless stated otherwise. Japanese fiscal year (JFY) runs April to March (JFY2027 = April 2027 to March 2028).
- "(secondary)" = trade press, aggregator or Wikipedia, not the mission owner or agency.
- "UNCERTAIN" = not verified from a primary or authoritative source in this session (seen only in a secondary source or in a search-result excerpt whose page was not opened).
- "Status as of <date>" labels any status that is older than October 2026.

## Q1. Claim check: "a Japanese company has managed to perform debris cleaning only 4 times"

### Takeaway
FALSE as worded. As of 26 October 2026, Astroscale (Tokyo) has removed zero pieces of real space debris, and so has every other company. Astroscale has flown two on-orbit missions. ELSA-d (2021 to 2024) captured its own purpose-built 17 kg "client" satellite, with one successful release-and-recapture demonstration on 25 August 2021. ADRAS-J (2024 to 2026) approached a real abandoned H-IIA rocket stage to about 15 m and photographed it without touching it. Astroscale itself says its next mission, ADRAS-J2 (launch targeted for JFY2027), "aims to become the world's first" to capture and remove an existing piece of large debris.

### Cited Findings
- Astroscale's 1 Sep 2026 release says ADRAS-J2 "aims to become the world's first mission to capture and remove an existing piece of large orbital debris". A footnote says this is "based on public information in July 2026". In other words, by Astroscale's own account nobody, Astroscale included, had captured and removed such an object by mid-2026. — [Astroscale Japan Selects Isar Aerospace to Launch ADRAS-J2](https://www.astroscale.com/en/news/astroscale-japan-selects-isar-aerospace-to-launch-adras-j2)
- Astroscale's 25 Mar 2026 release calls ADRAS-J "the world's first mission to successfully approach and capture close-range images of a large piece of actual space debris". Here "capture" means taking images, not grabbing the object. The release also describes ADRAS-J2 as a step toward "the world's first debris removal mission originating from Japan" (its footnote cites public information as of January 2026). — [Astroscale's ADRAS-J Mission Completes Operations, Begins Deorbit](https://astroscale.com/en/news/astroscales-adras-j-mission-completes-operations-begins-deorbit)
- ELSA-d was two spacecraft launched stacked together, a servicer and a client, using RPO and a magnetic Docking Plate. The thing it captured was its own client, not debris. — [ELSA-d Mission page, Astroscale](https://www.astroscale.com/en/missions/elsa-d)
- Only the first ELSA-d capture demonstration was completed, a manual magnetic docking after releasing the client. The later "Autonomous Capture demonstration" was not attempted because of anomalous spacecraft conditions. — [Astroscale's ELSA-d Finalizes De-Orbit Operations Marking Successful Mission Conclusion (24 Jan 2024)](https://www.astroscale.com/news/astroscales-elsa-d-finalizes-de-orbit-operations-marking-successful-mission-conclusion)
- Astroscale planned four ELSA-d demonstration types. The August 2021 release lists the completed first test and three more to come: "capture without tumbling", "capture with tumbling", and "diagnosis and client search". — [Astroscale's ELSA-d Successfully Demonstrates Repeated Magnetic Capture](https://www.astroscale.com/news/astroscales-elsa-d-successfully-demonstrates-repeated-magnetic-capture)
- Astroscale is described as offering four service lines: end-of-life (EOL), active debris removal (ADR), life extension (LEX), and in-situ space situational awareness (ISSA). — [Verdict](https://www.verdict.co.uk/orbital-debris-clearance-astroscale/) (secondary; search-result excerpt)
- An independent scorecard (published 26 Oct 2026, checked 21 Oct 2026) says: "No mission has yet captured and deorbited an uncooperative piece of debris in low Earth orbit." — [KeepTrack: Active Debris Removal Missions in 2026](https://keeptrack.space/deep-dive/active-debris-removal-scorecard) (secondary)

### Inferences
- The claim is false on both counts. The number of real debris objects Astroscale has removed is 0, not 4. Counting anything Astroscale has ever captured gives 1 event: ELSA-d recapturing its own client in August 2021. Counting launched missions gives 2 (ELSA-d and ADRAS-J).
- Where the number "4" might come from (my inference; no source states it this way):
  - ELSA-d had four planned demonstration types, of which only the first was completed.
  - Astroscale markets four service lines (EOL, ADR, LEX, ISSA).
  - A count of ADRAS-J operations, for example three fly-arounds plus the final 15 m approach.
  
  None of these is "debris cleaning".
- Deorbiting Astroscale's own spacecraft is self-disposal, not debris cleaning. This covers the ELSA-d servicer (finished January 2024) and ADRAS-J (lowering its orbit since March 2026).
- Suggested accurate statement (EN): "As of October 2026, no company, including Japan's Astroscale, has yet removed a real piece of space debris from orbit. Astroscale's ELSA-d (2021) magnetically captured its own purpose-built test satellite. ADRAS-J (2024) flew to within about 15 m of a real abandoned Japanese H-IIA rocket stage and photographed it without touching it. The first real capture-and-deorbit attempt, ADRAS-J2, is planned to launch between April 2027 and March 2028."
- The same statement in Russian, for the website (my translation): «По состоянию на октябрь 2026 года ни одна компания, включая японскую Astroscale, ещё не удалила с орбиты ни одного настоящего фрагмента космического мусора. Миссия ELSA-d (2021) магнитно захватила собственный специально построенный тестовый спутник, а ADRAS-J (2024) подлетела примерно на 15 м к реальной брошенной ступени японской ракеты H-IIA и сфотографировала её, не касаясь. Первая настоящая попытка захвата и увода с орбиты — миссия ADRAS-J2 — запланирована к запуску в период с апреля 2027 по март 2028 года.»

### Gaps
- No original source for the "4 times" claim was found. A search for the phrase together with Astroscale and debris removal turned up nothing relevant, so it is not known what the claim was based on.
- The August 2021 ELSA-d release title says "repeated" magnetic capture, but the text does not say how many release and capture cycles took place in that test.

## Q2. Astroscale ELSA-d (End-of-Life Services by Astroscale – demonstration), 2021–2024

### Takeaway
ELSA-d launched on 22 March 2021 from Baikonur and completed one short-range magnetic release-and-recapture of its own client on 25 August 2021. Its January 2022 autonomous capture attempt was called off after anomalies; four of its eight thrusters had failed. In April 2022 it re-approached the free-flying client to 159 m but never recaptured it. The servicer was lowered to about 500 km, with re-entry expected about 3.5 years later. Astroscale announced the end of the mission on 24 January 2024. ELSA-d never touched real debris.

### Cited Findings
- **Launch and preparation:**
  - Launch date 22 March 2021. The mission page's "Successful launch" news item is dated 23 March 2021.
  - Launch contract signed with Glavkosmos/GK Launch Services on 5 October 2018.
  - ELSA-d shipped to the launch site on 22 December 2020.
  - The UK Space Agency approved the mission licence on 12 March 2021.
  - Source: [ELSA-d Mission page](https://www.astroscale.com/en/missions/elsa-d)
- ELSA-d was launched "as a stack from the Baikonur Cosmodrome, Kazakhstan" in March 2021. — [ELSA-d Finalizes De-Orbit Operations (24 Jan 2024)](https://www.astroscale.com/news/astroscales-elsa-d-finalizes-de-orbit-operations-marking-successful-mission-conclusion)
- Masses: servicer about 175 kg, client about 17 kg; the client carries a ferromagnetic docking plate. — [SatNews, 18 Nov 2020](https://news.satnews.com/2020/11/18/astroscales-end-of-life-services-satellite-elsa-d-to-launch-in-march-of-2021/); [TechCrunch, 18 Nov 2020](https://techcrunch.com/2020/11/18/astroscale-sets-march-2021-for-first-commercial-orbital-debris-removal-demonstration) (secondary; masses seen in search-result excerpts)
- **First capture demonstration, 25 August 2021 (UTC).** The release is datelined Tokyo, 26 August 2021. The sequence was:
  1. The mechanical locking mechanism, which had held the two craft together since launch, was unlocked.
  2. The magnetic capture system alone then held the client.
  3. The client was "separated from the servicer for the first time and captured to validate the magnetic capture system".
  4. Rendezvous sensors were checked out and calibrated during the test.
  
  The release gives no separation distance. It said the next phase, "capture without tumbling", would separate the client to "a greater distance". — [Astroscale's ELSA-d Successfully Demonstrates Repeated Magnetic Capture](https://www.astroscale.com/news/astroscales-elsa-d-successfully-demonstrates-repeated-magnetic-capture)
- Astroscale's end-of-mission summary describes this first demonstration as the servicer releasing the client and "manually" performing magnetic docking, which validated the capture system, onboard sensors and cameras. — [ELSA-d Finalizes De-Orbit Operations](https://www.astroscale.com/news/astroscales-elsa-d-finalizes-de-orbit-operations-marking-successful-mission-conclusion)
- **Autonomous Capture demonstration, January 2022.** After releasing the client, the servicer held a "home position" using autonomous relative navigation for over seven hours. Anomalous spacecraft conditions were detected, so the team did not go ahead with the capture. — [ELSA-d Finalizes De-Orbit Operations](https://www.astroscale.com/news/astroscales-elsa-d-finalizes-de-orbit-operations-marking-successful-mission-conclusion)
- The pause was reported in the press on 27 January 2022. — [Via Satellite: "Astroscale pauses ELSA-d mission after detecting spacecraft anomaly"](https://www.satellitetoday.com/technology/2022/01/27/astroscale-pauses-elsa-d-mission-after-detecting-spacecraft-anomaly) (secondary; date taken from the URL and title)
- The hold distance was about 30 m station-keeping for over seven hours. — [KeepTrack](https://keeptrack.space/deep-dive/active-debris-removal-scorecard) (UNCERTAIN, secondary only)
- **Thruster failures.** Four of the eight thrusters failed: three because of a system issue, the fourth for an unknown reason. — [Via Satellite, 4 May 2022](https://www.satellitetoday.com/technology/2022/05/04/astroscale-elsa-d-mission-makes-close-approach-with-client-spacecraft) (secondary). Astroscale's January 2024 release also confirms "four of its eight thrusters" were lost.
- **Rendezvous, 7 April 2022** (announced 4 May 2022):
  - The servicer manoeuvred to 159 m from the client and detected it.
  - It handed over from absolute navigation (GPS plus ground observations) to relative navigation (onboard sensors), which Astroscale called "the most challenging operation of the ELSA-d mission so far".
  - The servicer then moved away; the autonomous capture demonstration was not completed.
  - The team said it would evaluate whether a safe magnetic recapture could be done.
  - Source: [Via Satellite, 4 May 2022](https://www.satellitetoday.com/technology/2022/05/04/astroscale-elsa-d-mission-makes-close-approach-with-client-spacecraft); the mission page lists "Successful completion of complex rendezvous operations" on 4 May 2022 ([ELSA-d Mission page](https://www.astroscale.com/en/missions/elsa-d)).
- **Capabilities Astroscale lists for the whole mission:**
  - Navigation "from about 1,600 km to within 160 m of the client" using GPS and ground-based observations.
  - Transition to relative navigation using an onboard LPR sensor.
  - Rendezvous with an uncontrolled object.
  - Autonomous GNC (guidance, navigation and control).
  - More than two years of in-orbit operations.
  - Source: [ELSA-d Finalizes De-Orbit Operations](https://www.astroscale.com/news/astroscales-elsa-d-finalizes-de-orbit-operations-marking-successful-mission-conclusion)
- After the thruster problems the two satellites kept orbiting separately, and the last mission update came in September 2022. — search-result excerpt (UNCERTAIN; the source page was not opened)
- **End of mission, 24 January 2024.** Astroscale completed a "safe and controlled de-orbit" of the servicer using its remaining thrusters.
  - The servicer is at about 500 km and "will re-enter and burn-up in approximately 3.5 years", well within the 25-year guideline.
  - The client "is predicted to naturally de-orbit within five years"; it cannot manoeuvre.
  - Source: [ELSA-d Finalizes De-Orbit Operations](https://www.astroscale.com/news/astroscales-elsa-d-finalizes-de-orbit-operations-marking-successful-mission-conclusion)
  - Conflict: a mirror of the release reportedly says the client will de-orbit "over the next 10 years". — [APSCC mirror](https://apscc.or.kr/astroscales-elsa-d-finalizes-de-orbit-operations-marking-successful-mission-conclusion/) (UNCERTAIN; seen only in a search-result excerpt)
- Astroscale's 2023 goal for finishing deorbit operations was missed; completion was announced in January 2024. — [Statement on ELSA-d De-Orbit Plan (Astroscale)](https://astroscale.com/?p=7371) (UNCERTAIN; search-result excerpt only, the page redirected when opened)
- The mission page's current status is "Mission complete". Astroscale bills ELSA-d as "The world's first commercial mission to demonstrate the core technologies necessary for debris docking and removal". — [ELSA-d Mission page](https://www.astroscale.com/en/missions/elsa-d)

### Inferences
- Count of ELSA-d capture events:
  - 1 successful release-and-recapture demonstration (25 August 2021, short range, manually commanded).
  - 0 autonomous captures.
  - The client was released in January 2022 and was never recaptured.
  - 1 long-range re-approach to 159–160 m (7 April 2022).
- Expected re-entries: the servicer around mid-2027 (24 January 2024 plus about 3.5 years) and the client by about January 2029 (within five years). Both are therefore probably still in orbit in October 2026. This is my inference and was not checked against tracking data.
- "Debris" in ELSA-d was a cooperative stand-in: purpose-built, launched with the servicer, and fitted with a docking plate.

### Gaps
- The exact launch vehicle variant (commonly reported as Soyuz-2.1a/Fregat) and the co-passengers were not verified in a primary source this session. The Astroscale page names only Glavkosmos/GK Launch Services (Russia's commercial Soyuz provider) and the release names Baikonur.
- The exact January 2022 date of the autonomous-capture attempt and the hold distance were not confirmed from a primary source.
- The pre-deorbit altitude and the number and dates of deorbit burns were not published in the January 2024 release.

## Q3. Astroscale ADRAS-J (Active Debris Removal by Astroscale–Japan), JAXA CRD2 Phase I, 2024–2026

### Takeaway
ADRAS-J launched on 18 February 2024 (14:52 UTC) on a Rocket Lab Electron from New Zealand. Over 2024 it did the following to the 2009 H-IIA upper stage (catalog 33500):
- approached from several hundred km;
- held at 50 m (from 23 May 2024);
- flew around it (15 and 16 July and 13 August 2024);
- reached 20 m on 17 July 2024;
- reached about 15 m below the payload attach fitting on 30 November 2024, when an autonomous abort was triggered.

It never captured or touched the stage. Deorbit operations began on 25 March 2026.

### Cited Findings
- **Launch:** 18 February 2024 at 14:52 UTC (03:52 NZDT, 19 February), on mission "On Closer Inspection", Rocket Lab's 44th Electron. It flew from Pad B, Launch Complex 1, Mahia, New Zealand. ADRAS-J was deployed 64 minutes after liftoff following two Curie kick-stage burns. — [Rocket Lab press release](https://rocketlabcorp.com/updates/rocket-lab-successfully-launches-mission-designed-to-investigate-removing-space-junk-from-orbit/); [Spaceflight Now, 18 Feb 2024](https://spaceflightnow.com/2024/02/18/rocket-lab-electron-rocket-to-launch-space-debris-removal-mission/) (launch facts from search-result excerpts)
- **Spacecraft mass:** about 150 kg. — [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2024/02/18/rocket-lab-electron-rocket-to-launch-space-debris-removal-mission/) (secondary; search-result excerpt)
- **Target identity:** the H-IIA upper stage ("H2A R/B") that launched GOSAT "Ibuki" in 2009. International designator 2009-002J, catalog number 33500, in orbit for about 15 years at the time. — [JAXA press release, 30 Jul 2024](https://global.jaxa.jp/press/2024/07/20240730-1_e.html)
- **Target size:** about 11 m long, 4 m in diameter, about 3 tons; non-cooperative. — [Astroscale, 25 Mar 2026](https://astroscale.com/en/news/astroscales-adras-j-mission-completes-operations-begins-deorbit); [Astroscale, 1 Sep 2026](https://www.astroscale.com/en/news/astroscale-japan-selects-isar-aerospace-to-launch-adras-j2)
- **Target orbit:** about 600 km. — [KeepTrack](https://keeptrack.space/deep-dive/active-debris-removal-scorecard) (secondary)
- **Official timeline** — [ADRAS-J Mission page](https://www.astroscale.com/en/missions/adras-j):
  - 12 Feb 2020: Astroscale selected as JAXA's CRD2 commercial partner.
  - 18 Feb 2024: launch and start of on-orbit operations.
  - 22 Feb 2024: start of rendezvous phase.
  - 9 Apr 2024: angles-only navigation begins ("proximity approach from several hundred kilometers").
  - 16 Apr 2024: model-matching navigation begins.
  - 17 Apr 2024: approach to within several hundred metres.
  - 23 May 2024: approach to within 50 m; first fixed-point observation.
  - 17 Jun 2024: second fixed-point observation.
  - 19 Jun 2024: start of fly-around operation and collision-avoidance validation.
  - 14 Jul 2024: third fixed-point observation, within 50 m.
  - 15 Jul 2024: first fly-around observation.
  - 16 Jul 2024: second fly-around observation.
  - 17 Jul 2024: first final-approach attempt, reaching 20 m.
  - 13 Aug 2024: third fly-around observation.
  - 30 Nov 2024: final approach, reaching 15 m.
  - 25 Mar 2026: start of deorbit operations.
  
  The page's status line reads "Phase I of CRD2 completed".
- **First image release (JAXA, 26 April 2024):**
  - The image was taken from several hundred metres behind the debris.
  - It confirmed a vertical, nadir-aligned posture, consistent with JAXA's modelling and ground observations.
  - It showed that orange insulation had darkened to dark brown, as predicted from UV tests.
  - JAXA said understanding the stage's attitude motion is "crucial for the upcoming capture in CRD2 Phase II".
  - Source: [JAXA press release, 26 Apr 2024](https://global.jaxa.jp/press/2024/04/20240426-2_e.html)
- **Fly-around images (JAXA, 30 July 2024):** taken from about 50 m on 15 and 16 July 2024. JAXA says the images were released by Astroscale Japan Inc. — [JAXA, 30 Jul 2024](https://global.jaxa.jp/press/2024/07/20240730-1_e.html)
- **The 15 m approach (Astroscale release, 11 December 2024):**
  - On 30 November 2024 ADRAS-J manoeuvred to "approximately 15 meters below the Payload Attach Fitting (PAF)", the planned ADRAS-J2 capture point, matching relative speed, distance and attitude.
  - The onboard collision-avoidance system then triggered an autonomous abort because of an "unexpected relative attitude anomaly with the upper stage". The spacecraft moved away before reaching the Capture Initiation Point (CIP), and the cause was under investigation.
  - The CIP approach was Astroscale's own goal, beyond JAXA's baseline; all JAXA-required observations had been completed.
  - Astroscale calls it the "closest approach to space debris by a commercial company using RPO".
  - Two fly-arounds confirmed no major damage to the PAF.
  - Source: [Astroscale's ADRAS-J Achieves Historic 15-Meter Approach to Space Debris](https://astroscale.com/en/news/astroscales-adras-j-achieves-historic-15-meter-approach-to-space-debris)
- **End of mission (Astroscale release, 25 March 2026):**
  - "293 days of mission life in orbit".
  - Milestones: long-range approach, imaging, fly-arounds, and validation of autonomous collision avoidance; one abort followed by safe recovery and "multiple successful approaches"; closest approach within 15 m; imaged the PAF.
  - It has lowered its orbit enough for natural decay and re-entry within five years, will continue lowering it, and will eventually burn up.
  - The debris was not physically captured.
  - Source: [Astroscale's ADRAS-J Mission Completes Operations, Begins Deorbit](https://astroscale.com/en/news/astroscales-adras-j-mission-completes-operations-begins-deorbit)
- Astroscale states it became "the first commercial company to rendezvous with and conduct close-proximity inspection of an existing large piece of orbital debris". The PAF showed no significant damage, which informed capture planning. — [Astroscale, 1 Sep 2026](https://www.astroscale.com/en/news/astroscale-japan-selects-isar-aerospace-to-launch-adras-j2)
- The IAC 2025 highlight lecture "ADRAS-J: First Encounter with Space Debris" presents the mission as successful. — [IAF](https://www.iafastro.org/events/iac/international-astronautical-congress-2025/plenary-programme/highlight-lectures/adras-j-first-encounter-with-space-debris.html) (search-result excerpt)

### Inferences
- The 293 days counted from 18 February 2024 end around 6–7 December 2024, so "mission life" covers the active proximity-operations phase. The spacecraft then stayed in orbit until deorbit began in March 2026.
- ADRAS-J removed nothing. Its real achievement is the first commercial rendezvous with, and close inspection of, a real large uncooperative object, one that broadcasts no GPS or telemetry and has no docking aids.
- Re-entry "within five years" of March 2026 puts the latest natural re-entry around 2031.

### Gaps
- No exact altitudes were published for ADRAS-J before or after its deorbit burns.
- No root cause for the 30 November 2024 abort was found in any published source; it was "under investigation" in December 2024.
- A reported earlier autonomous abort during a July 2024 fly-around (about 120° into the circuit) appeared only in a search-result excerpt and is not verified.

## Q4. ADRAS-J2 (JAXA CRD2 Phase II): planned capture and deorbit of the same H-IIA stage

### Takeaway
As of 26 October 2026, ADRAS-J2 is still under development. It is contracted by JAXA (about ¥13.2 billion) to grab the same 2009 H-IIA upper stage at its payload attach fitting with a robotic arm and deorbit it. On 1 September 2026 Isar Aerospace's Spectrum was announced as the rocket, launching from Andøya, Norway, with launch targeted for JFY2027 (April 2027 to March 2028).

### Cited Findings
- **Contract (Astroscale ADRAS-J2 mission page):**
  - Selected for CRD2 Phase II on 25 April 2024; contract signed 20 August 2024, worth about 13.2 billion yen including tax; customer JAXA.
  - Target: an "unprepared Japanese upper stage rocket body" with no features that enable docking, servicing or removal.
  - Method: approach by RPO, then "remove and deorbit the rocket body using in-house robotic arm technologies".
  - Status: "Under development".
  - Source: [ADRAS-J2 Mission page](https://www.astroscale.com/en/missions/adras-j2)
- **Launch (1 September 2026 release, Tokyo/Munich):** Isar Aerospace Spectrum from the company's pad at Andøya Space, Norway, targeted for "Japan's fiscal year 2027 (April 2027 to March 2028)". The target is the non-cooperative upper stage, about 11 m × 4 m and about 3 t. — [Astroscale Japan Selects Isar Aerospace to Launch ADRAS-J2](https://www.astroscale.com/en/news/astroscale-japan-selects-isar-aerospace-to-launch-adras-j2); also [Isar Aerospace press release](https://isaraerospace.com/press/isar-aerospace-signs-launch-contract-with-astroscale-japan) (not opened)
- **Capture point:** the Payload Attach Fitting (PAF), which ADRAS-J inspected from about 15 m. — [Astroscale, 11 Dec 2024](https://astroscale.com/en/news/astroscales-adras-j-achieves-historic-15-meter-approach-to-space-debris)
- **Schedule conflict:** a company profile gives the launch as "the company's fiscal year 2028". — [Venture Atlas](https://www.ventureatlas.org/company/astroscale) (secondary; conflicts with Astroscale's primary statement of JFY2027)
- **Launcher record:** Spectrum's first flight, in March 2025, failed seconds after liftoff because of loss of attitude control. — [European Spaceflight](https://europeanspaceflight.com/?p=6218) (secondary)
- **Build progress:** one report says capture-mechanism vibration tests at Tsukuba were complete and satellite AIT had begun. — search-result excerpt (UNCERTAIN; could not be verified)
- **Older schedules:** earlier media headlines project a 2026 robotic-arm capture, for example [Space.com: "Astroscale will capture old space junk with robotic arm in 2026"](https://www.space.com/astroscale-space-junk-removal-2026-plan-exclusive-video). These are superseded.

### Inferences
- If it succeeds, ADRAS-J2 would be the first robotic capture and removal of a large real piece of debris in LEO. Even on schedule, the launch would come no earlier than April 2027, and capture would follow months of rendezvous operations (ADRAS-J took about 9 months to reach 15 m).
- Schedule risk is tied partly to Spectrum's flight record (my inference).

### Gaps
- The status of Spectrum's second flight (a scrubbed attempt versus a reported success) is contradictory in the search results and was not resolved.
- Whether the final re-entry will be controlled or uncontrolled, and the deorbit timeline after capture, are not stated in the sources opened.

## Q5. Other Astroscale missions (ELSA-M, ISSA-J1, LEXI-P): status as of October 2026

### Takeaway
None of these has launched.
- **ELSA-M** (UK, removal of a prepared Eutelsat OneWeb satellite by magnetic docking) is under development, has slipped from 2026 to "FY2028 or later", and will fly on Isar Spectrum.
- **ISSA-J1** (Japan, inspection only, no contact) is scheduled for 2027. It will visit two dead Japanese satellites, ALOS "Daichi" and ADEOS-II "Midori-II".
- **LEXI-P** (a GEO life-extension servicer) is listed as under development, with no public customer or launch date.

### Cited Findings
- **ELSA-M mission page:**
  - Customers: Eutelsat, ESA, UK Space Agency.
  - Purpose: "harnessing magnetic capture for multiple satellite removals" of "prepared in-orbit client satellites" (RPO plus Docking Plate).
  - Phase 3 kick-off June 2024; Phase 4 (flight-model AIT) kick-off June 2025.
  - Status: "Under development".
  - Source: [ELSA-M Mission page](https://www.astroscale.com/en/missions/elsa-m)
  - Note: as extracted, the page also pairs "CDR complete" with 16 Mar 2026. That conflicts with June 2025 CDR reports below and may be an extraction error (UNCERTAIN).
- **Isar launch selection, 16 March 2026:** Astroscale Ltd, the UK subsidiary, selected Isar Aerospace. ELSA-M is a 520 kg servicer that "will capture and remove an end-of-life Eutelsat OneWeb satellite". It is supported by the UK Space Agency through ESA's ARTES programme as part of the Sunrise Partnership Project with Eutelsat; "the majority of the mission is self-funded by Astroscale". The release gives no launch date. — [Astroscale Selects Isar Aerospace to Launch ELSA-M In-Orbit Demonstration](https://www.astroscale.com/news/astroscale-selects-isar-aerospace-to-launch-elsa-m-in-orbit-demonstration); [Isar Aerospace release, 16 Mar 2026](https://isaraerospace.com/press/isar-aerospace-secures-first-active-debris-removal-mission-with-astroscale) (search-result excerpt: Spectrum from Andøya)
- **ELSA-M launch timing:** Astroscale is quoted as expecting launch on Spectrum in "FY2028 or later". It was still giving 2026 as recently as June 2025. — [European Spaceflight, 13 Mar 2026](https://europeanspaceflight.com/?p=6218) (secondary, quoting Astroscale)

## Q6. ESA ClearSpace-1: original target hit by debris, new target PROBA-1, status in 2026

### Takeaway
ClearSpace-1 has not flown.
- Its original target, the 113 kg VESPA adapter left by Vega flight VV02 in 2013, was apparently struck by a small untracked object. ESA was told of new fragments on 10 August 2023.
- In April 2024 the mission was re-planned: the new target is ESA's 95 kg PROBA-1 satellite (launched 2001), OHB became prime, and a launch on Vega C in the second half of 2026 was then planned.
- ESA's ClearSpace-1 page now lists the launch as 2029.

### Cited Findings
- **The VESPA event (ESA, 22 August 2023):**
  - On 10 August 2023 ESA's Space Debris Office was told that new objects had been detected near VESPA. They were first reported by the US 18th Space Defense Squadron and later observed independently by Fraunhofer FHR's TIRA radar and the Polish "European Optical Network".
  - The most likely cause was the hypervelocity impact of a small, untracked object, which released a low-energy cloud of fragments.
  - The main object remained intact with no significant orbit change, and the added collision risk to other missions was assessed as negligible.
  - Source: [ESA: Objects detected in the vicinity of ClearSpace-1 debris removal mission target](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/ClearSpace-1/Objects_detected_in_the_vicinity_of_ClearSpace-1_debris_removal_mission_target)
- **What VESPA is:** a 113 kg, 2 m-diameter conical upper part of a payload adapter, left in orbit after the 2013 Vega VV02 launch (Proba-V, VNREDSat-1, ESTCube-1). Orbit: 660 km × 790 km, inclination 98.72°. — [ESA, 22 Aug 2023](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/ClearSpace-1/Objects_detected_in_the_vicinity_of_ClearSpace-1_debris_removal_mission_target)
- **Re-plan (announced 24 April 2024):**
  - After the first phase (Key Performance Gate 1), the target changed to PROBA-1, "the first ESA spacecraft with fully autonomous capabilities".
  - OHB SE leads the consortium, providing the bus, integration and launch; ClearSpace leads close-proximity and capture operations.
  - Plan: rendezvous, capture, then a perigee-lowering manoeuvre so the stack re-enters and burns up.
  - Launch then planned for the second half of 2026 on Vega C (Arianespace) from Kourou.
  - Source: [ClearSpace: ClearSpace-1 Mission Changes in Response to Space Debris Collision to go Leaner, Faster and Safer](https://clearspace.today/news/clearspace-1-mission-changes-in-response-to-space-debris-collision-to-go-leaner-faster-and-safer) (status as of April 2024)
- **ESA's ClearSpace-1 page today:** PROBA-1 launched in 2001, 95 kg, 0.6 × 0.6 × 0.8 m, in LEO; OHB SE leads the industrial team with ClearSpace; planned launch 2029; in development. The newest dated story on the page is "ESA advances Clearspace-1 development" (23 April 2024). — [ESA ClearSpace-1](https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/ClearSpace-1)
- **Conflicting launch years elsewhere:** 2028 on Wikipedia and on ClearSpace's own site (which also mentions a €100M total budget to launch). — [Wikipedia: ClearSpace-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ClearSpace-1); [ClearSpace](https://clearspace.today/) (UNCERTAIN; search-result excerpts). ESA's 2029 is the authoritative current figure.
- **Other context:** original contract December 2020, €86M; PROBA-1 orbit roughly 500–580 km; slip of about four years. — [KeepTrack](https://keeptrack.space/deep-dive/active-debris-removal-scorecard) (secondary)

### Inferences
- The VESPA strike is a powerful real-world example for a collision-risk website: the target of a debris-removal mission was itself apparently hit by untracked debris before the mission could fly.
- Note on terminology: PROBA-1 is a retired ESA satellite with no docking fixtures, so it is uncooperative in the GNC sense. But ESA owns it and knows it well, so it is a more benign target than the original VESPA.

### Gaps
- No 2025–2026 ESA press release explaining the move from "2H 2026" to 2029 was found.
- The VESPA fragment count was not stated by ESA.

## Q7. Other demonstrations: RemoveDEBRIS (2018–2019), Northrop Grumman MEV-1/MEV-2, plus context (China's SJ-21, Space Shuttle retrievals)

### Takeaway
- **RemoveDEBRIS** (University of Surrey/SSTL/Airbus) netted its own released inflatable target on 16 September 2018 and harpooned a panel on its own 1.5 m boom on 8 February 2019. Its drag sail failed to deploy in March 2019.
- **MEV-1** docked with Intelsat 901 on 25 February 2020 and undocked on 9 April 2025 after returning it to a graveyard orbit. **MEV-2** docked with Intelsat 10-02 on 12 April 2021. These were life-extension services to live satellites, not debris removal.
- **China's SJ-21** (January 2022) docked with the dead BeiDou-2 G2 and towed it out of GEO to a graveyard orbit. This was relocation, not removal from orbit.

### Cited Findings
- **RemoveDEBRIS timeline** — [Wikipedia: RemoveDEBRIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RemoveDEBRIS) (secondary):
  - Launched 2 April 2018 (20:30:38 UTC) on Falcon 9 inside Dragon CRS-14; reached the ISS on 4 April.
  - Deployed from Kibo via Canadarm2 on 20 June 2018; about 100 kg.
  - Net: on 16 September 2018 it captured DebrisSat-1 (DS-1), an inflatable debris simulator it had released itself, "from a short distance away".
  - Vision-based navigation: on 28 October 2018 DS-2 was released at 06:15 UTC; 361 images were taken.
  - Harpoon: 8 February 2019, fired at 20 m/s into a target on a 1.5 m boom.
  - Drag sail: targeted for 4 March 2019; failed to deploy.
  - Decay: 4 December 2021 (UNCERTAIN, infobox only).
- **Press-release corroboration:** "Net successfully snares space debris" (2018) and "Harpoon successfully captures space debris" (2019). The harpoon target was a piece of satellite panel on the end of a 1.5 m boom, hit at 20 m/s. — [Lab News](https://labnews.co.uk/net_successfully_snares_space_debris/); [ISISPACE](https://www.isispace.nl/harpoon-successfully-captures-space-debris/)
- **Drag-sail failure:** Surrey's Guglielmo Aglietti acknowledged that the RemoveDEBRIS sail did not deploy as expected and said lessons were applied to later sails. — [ADS Group: "Surrey-built space sail successfully deployed"](https://www.adsgroup.org.uk/knowledge/industry-member-news/surrey-built-space-sail-successfully-deployed/) (search-result excerpt)
- **Peer-reviewed reference:** G.S. Aglietti, B. Taylor, S. Fellowes et al., "RemoveDEBRIS: An in-orbit demonstration of technologies for the removal of space debris", *The Aeronautical Journal* 124 (2020), pp. 1–23, doi:10.1017/aer.2019.136. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/88B966915E7A3BD6F0B047A38FF713D2/core-reader) (not opened in full)
- **RemoveDEBRIS targets:** the targets were self-supplied (a CubeSat and a boom-mounted panel), not real debris. — [KeepTrack](https://keeptrack.space/deep-dive/active-debris-removal-scorecard) (secondary)
- **MEV-1:**
  - Docked with Intelsat 901 on 25 February 2020 (07:15 UTC), the first docking of two commercial satellites. IS-901 had been moved to a graveyard orbit above GEO in late 2019; MEV-1 docked there and returned it to service.
  - Undocked on 9 April 2025 (15:04 UTC) after moving IS-901 back to the graveyard orbit. MEV-1 was then reported heading to service Optus D3.
  - Source: [Wikipedia: Intelsat 901](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelsat_901); [Wikipedia: Mission Extension Vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_Extension_Vehicle) (secondary)
  - Intelsat's release, "Intelsat Completes Satellite Life-Extension Mission, Makes Space History", says IS-901 was decommissioned after nearly 24 years. — [Intelsat release via Seeking Alpha](https://seekingalpha.com/pr/20062184-intelsat-completes-satellite-life-extension-mission-makes-space-history) (search-result excerpt)
  - Additional reference on the first docking: [MIT Technology Review](https://cdn.technologyreview.com/f/615297/two-commercial-satellites-docked-in-orbit-for-the-first-time-ever)
- **MEV-2:** docked with Intelsat 10-02 on 12 April 2021 at 13:34 US Eastern (17:34 UTC), directly in its operational GEO slot; the first docking with an in-service commercial GEO satellite. It is contracted for five years of service before undocking. — [Via Satellite, 12 Apr 2021](https://www.satellitetoday.com/technology/2021/04/12/northrop-grumman-successfully-docks-second-mission-extension-vehicle-with-operational-intelsat-satellite/); [Wikipedia: Intelsat 10-02](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelsat_10-02) (lists undocking as "2026 (planned)")
- **SJ-21 and BeiDou-2 G2:** in January 2022, Shijian-21 pulled the defunct BeiDou-G2 out of the GEO region into a "graveyard orbit"; its rotation state had to be determined in advance for safe docking. — [arXiv 2204.09258, Hou-Yuan Lin: "BeiDou-G2: Past and Present" (Apr 2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.09258)
  - Further reported details, all from search-result excerpts: undocking on 26 January 2022 per US 18th Space Control Squadron data; final orbit roughly 3,000 km above GEO (some reports say about 300 km); BeiDou-2 G2 launched in 2009 and failed on reaching orbit; China disclosed little. — [Britannica: Shijian 21](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Shijian-21); [eoPortal: Shijian-21](https://eoportal.org/satellite-missions/shijian-21) (UNCERTAIN)
- **Space Shuttle STS-51-A (Discovery), 8–16 November 1984:**
  - Retrieved two failed communications satellites, Palapa B2 and Westar VI. Both had been stranded in useless low orbits after their Payload Assist Module motors failed when deployed from STS-41B in February 1984.
  - Astronauts Joseph Allen and Dale Gardner used Manned Maneuvering Units; Anna Fisher operated the robotic arm; the Palapa EVA was on 12 November 1984.
  - Both satellites were returned to Earth, reportedly refurbished and reflown.
  - Source: [NASA: STS-51A](https://www.nasa.gov/mission/sts-51a); [Spacefacts STS-51A](https://www.spacefacts.de/mission/english/sts-51a.htm) (search-result excerpt)
- **Starfish Space Otter Pup 2:**
  - Launched 23 June 2025 on Transporter-14 to attempt docking with a D-Orbit ION vehicle, which is operational, not debris.
  - Earlier, on 19 April 2024, Otter Pup 1 flew by an ION within about 1 km.
  - Source: [Wikipedia: Starfish Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starfish_Space) (secondary)

### Inferences
- RemoveDEBRIS and ELSA-d are the two flown LEO demonstrations of capture hardware. Both used targets they carried up themselves.
- MEV-1/2 proved docking with satellites not designed for servicing. But the clients were operational, attitude-controlled satellites, not tumbling debris, and the aim was life extension.
- SJ-21 is the closest real-world precedent for capturing a dead satellite with a robotic servicer. It happened in GEO and moved the object to a disposal orbit rather than removing it from space.
- The 1984 Shuttle retrieval is a crewed salvage of two derelict satellites, an important historical footnote, but not a "dedicated debris removal mission".

## Q8. Has ANY uncooperative piece of real debris ever been captured and removed by a dedicated mission (as of 26 October 2026)?

### Takeaway
No, in the strict sense. As of 26 October 2026, no dedicated robotic mission has captured a real, uncooperative piece of orbital debris that was not prepared for capture and removed it from orbit (deorbited it). The near-misses:
- **ADRAS-J** came within about 15 m of a real rocket stage (2024) with no contact.
- **ELSA-d and RemoveDEBRIS** captured targets they brought themselves.
- **MEV-1/2** docked with live satellites.
- **China's SJ-21** captured a dead BeiDou satellite in GEO but only moved it to a graveyard orbit (January 2022).

The first planned true capture-and-deorbit attempts are ADRAS-J2 (launch JFY2027), ClearSpace-1 (2029), the UK ADR mission (operations 2029) and South Korea's ADR satellite (2027, UNCERTAIN).
